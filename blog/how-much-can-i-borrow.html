Borrowing Power

How Much Can I Borrow for a Mortgage in NZ?

Three things set the limit: whether you can afford the repayments at a higher test rate, how your total debt compares with your income, and how much deposit you have. The lowest of the three is your answer.

The three limits

  1. Affordability. The lender adds up your income, subtracts your living costs and existing debts, and checks that what's left covers the repayments at a test rate set higher than the rate you'd actually pay.
  2. Debt-to-income (DTI). Reserve Bank rules limit how much lending banks can do above six times income for owner-occupiers and seven times for investors. On a household income of $150,000, six times is $900,000 of total debt.
  3. Deposit (LVR). Most lending is capped at 80% of the property's value for owner-occupiers, which means a 20% deposit. Lending above that exists but is limited.

You can check two of these yourself with the DTI calculator and the LVR calculator. The borrowing power calculator gives a rough overall figure.

Why a simple income multiple misleads

Rules of thumb such as five times your income ignore everything that makes households different. Two couples on the same income can be offered very different amounts if one has two children, a car loan and a $15,000 credit card limit and the other has none of those. The affordability test, not the multiple, is what decides most applications.

What counts as income

IncomeHow lenders usually treat it
Salary or wagesCounted in full
Overtime, bonuses, commissionOften counted in part, and usually only with a track record
Self-employed incomeUsually based on your last two years of financial statements
Rent from an investment propertyA percentage is counted, to allow for costs and vacancies
Boarder or flatmate incomeSome lenders count it within limits, others don't

Treatment differs between lenders, which is one reason borrowing capacity does too. See variable income and boarder income for the detail.

What reduces it

  • Credit card and overdraft limits. Lenders assess the limit, not the balance, as if it were fully drawn.
  • Car loans, personal loans and buy now, pay later. The repayments come straight off what you can afford.
  • Student loans. The compulsory repayment reduces your take-home pay. See student loans and borrowing power.
  • Dependants and living costs. Lenders use your declared spending or their own minimum for your household size, whichever is higher.

Ways to lift your borrowing power

  • Cut credit card limits you don't use, and close accounts you don't need.
  • Pay off small debts before you apply, if you can do it without draining your deposit.
  • Keep three months of tidy bank statements: no unarranged overdrafts or missed payments.
  • Look at new builds, which are exempt from the DTI and LVR limits.
  • Compare lenders. Each uses its own test rate, expense assumptions and income rules, so the same application can produce noticeably different amounts.

A worked example

Illustration onlyA couple earn $150,000 between them, have a $10,000 credit card limit and no other debt, and have saved $160,000. The DTI limit allows about $900,000 of debt. A 20% deposit limits the purchase to $800,000, a loan of $640,000. If the affordability test supports that loan, the deposit is what sets their budget. With children or a car loan, affordability would become the limit instead.

For a figure you can rely on when making an offer, get a pre-approval.
