Refinancing to Consolidate Debt: When It Helps and When It Hurts

Rolling credit cards, car finance and personal loans into your mortgage cuts your repayments straight away. Whether it saves money depends on what you do next.

How it works

You increase your home loan by the amount of your other debts, and the lender pays those debts off directly. Five repayments become one. Because home loan rates are usually far lower than credit card or personal loan rates, the monthly cost drops, often by a lot.

The trap

A personal loan runs for maybe five years. A mortgage runs for 25 or 30. Move $30,000 of short-term debt onto the mortgage and repay it at mortgage pace, and you can pay more interest in total than you would have at the higher rate, simply because you're paying it for so long.

The second trap is behavioural. The credit cards are back at zero and the temptation is obvious. A fair number of people who consolidate end up with the bigger mortgage and the card debt again within a couple of years. We're blunt about that with clients because it's the thing that decides whether consolidation was a good idea.

Doing it properly

  • Put the consolidated amount in its own loan split with a short term, say three to five years. You still get the lower rate, but the debt is gone on roughly the original timetable.
  • Or keep your total repayments the same as before. The money that used to go to the car loan now attacks the mortgage.
  • Close or cut the limits on the cards you've cleared. Lenders count card limits against your borrowing power anyway, so it helps future applications too.

What lenders look at

A lender will check that you have the equity, generally keeping the total loan within 80% of your home's value, and that you can afford the new loan. They'll also read your statements. Missed payments, unarranged overdrafts or buy-now-pay-later debt piling up make banks cautious, and some will decline even where consolidation would obviously improve your position.

If a bank says no, a non-bank lender may still help, at a higher rate, as a stepping stone back to a bank once things are tidy. And if you're already behind on payments, talk to your lender about hardship options or to a free budgeting service such as MoneyTalks before borrowing more.

Debt consolidation: common questions

Sometimes. Main banks are cautious about missed payments and defaults. Some non-bank lenders will consolidate debts for borrowers with credit problems if there's enough equity, usually at a higher rate. It should be a short-term step with a plan to move back to a bank.

Applying adds an enquiry to your file. After that, paying off and closing other accounts and keeping one loan up to date generally helps over time.

There's no set figure. It comes down to your equity and whether the lender is satisfied you can afford the new loan. Lenders may also question a pattern of repeated consolidation.
